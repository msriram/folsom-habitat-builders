Connecting habitat for golden lion tamarins

Our project investigates how a bridge could reconnect trees separated by gaps. We propose a modular bridge with intermediate pylons for longer spans, including a concept for roads or developed areas. Our PLA tabletop model demonstrates the idea; it does not establish that PLA or this structure is suitable for a full-size wildlife crossing.

Build and test

Start with the fit-test piece, then assemble links, mesh, supports and landing. Record span, sag and behavior under the same small test load. Compare one change at a time. Photograph failures as well as improvements.

Research existing canopy bridges. Ask an expert about tamarin movement, safe grip, gaps, anchors, pylon spacing, weathering, maintenance and material choice. A road crossing also needs appropriate engineering and landowner input.

Biodiversity basics

Useful ideas to know before choosing a problem

Species

Different kinds of living things have different jobs. Losing one species can change food, shelter, pollination, or the balance of an ecosystem.

Habitats

A habitat provides food, water, shelter, space, and the right conditions for a living thing. Even small changes can make a habitat harder to use.

Connections

Food webs, migration routes, soil, water, plants, and people are connected. A strong project explains at least one of those connections.

Native and invasive

Native species evolved in an area. An invasive species can spread quickly and compete with native species or change the habitat.

Resilience

An ecosystem is more resilient when it can handle drought, disease, fire, storms, or other changes and still recover.

Evidence

Observations are a starting point. Good research adds reliable sources, data, maps, interviews, and careful comparisons.

Concern areas

Where students can make an impact

These are directions to investigate, not pre-chosen answers. Pick one that feels important and can be explored with real people and evidence.

Habitat loss and fragmentation

Roads, fences, construction, and missing native plants can split a habitat into smaller pieces. Investigate how a local animal, pollinator, or plant loses safe space or a travel path.

Possible vector: help people notice, map, restore, or connect small habitat spaces.

Pollinators and native plants

Bees, butterflies, moths, birds, and bats need food and shelter throughout the year. Many yards and public spaces have gaps in native flowering plants.

Possible vector: make planting choices easier, clearer, or more measurable.

Water quality and watersheds

Storm drains, creeks, ponds, and wetlands connect neighborhoods to wildlife habitat. Trash, runoff, erosion, and warm water can affect many species.

Possible vector: help people prevent a problem, observe it safely, or understand where water travels.

Invasive species

Some plants, insects, fish, and animals spread into new places and crowd out native life. Different places need different responses.

Possible vector: improve identification, reporting, prevention, or public understanding—without handling wildlife.

Climate, heat, and drought

Hotter days, less water, smoke, and changing seasons can affect when plants flower and when animals find food, shade, or nesting places.

Possible vector: identify a local stress point and test a way to help people plan or care for habitat.

Light, noise, and human activity

Bright night lighting, noise, loose pets, and busy recreation can disturb animals even when a habitat still looks green.

Possible vector: make a behavior change easier, safer, or more visible for people.

Wildlife data gaps

Scientists and land managers cannot watch every species or location. Community observations can help when they are collected carefully.

Possible vector: create a simple observation tool, checklist, map, or reporting routine with an expert’s guidance.

Restoration and stewardship

Removing invasive plants, planting natives, protecting soil, and caring for water can help habitats recover. The challenge is often long-term follow-through.

Possible vector: make stewardship easier to organize, teach, track, or celebrate.

How to build a strong idea

1

Identify

Write one clear problem sentence: who or what is affected, what is happening, where, and why it matters.

2

Research

Use several source types: a government, university, museum, nonprofit, scientific organization, and a local expert when possible.

3

Compare

Find existing solutions. Ask: what works, what is hard, who is left out, and what could be improved?

4

Prototype

Make something testable: a model, mock-up, guide, map, experiment, app screen, kit, or better process.

5

Improve

Show the idea to relevant people. Record what they said and change something specific because of the feedback.
